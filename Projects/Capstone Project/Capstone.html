Design and Optimisation of Photonic Cavity Architectures for Quantum-Dot Single-Photon Sources

Welcome to my MSc capstone research project at the University of Bristol! This was a collaborative research project investigating the design, simulation, and optimisation of photonic cavity architectures for quantum-dot single-photon sources. The aim of the project was to investigate how different photonic structures could enhance spontaneous emission and improve the extraction of individual photons from semiconductor quantum dots. Reliable single-photon sources are an important component of emerging quantum technologies, particularly quantum computing, quantum communication, and quantum networks.

The research considered five photonic architectures: Circular Bragg Gratings (CBGs), micropillars, open cavities, photonic crystal waveguides, and hybrid Bragg-ring micropillar structures. Each architecture was evaluated using a common framework of performance metrics, including the Purcell factor, quality factor, photon extraction efficiency, single-photon purity, and indistinguishability. This allowed us to investigate the different advantages and limitations of each structure, rather than simply identifying whichever design produced the greatest optical enhancement.

My primary responsibility was the Circular Bragg Grating (CBG) architecture, which became the focus of Chapter 6 of our final research report. CBGs use concentric etched trenches surrounding a central emitter region to control the propagation of light within a photonic membrane. By carefully designing the grating geometry, light emitted by a quantum dot can be redirected vertically, improving the collection of individual photons. I was responsible for developing the simulation models, validating the reference structure, conducting parameter sweeps, and investigating potential improvements to the architecture using Ansys Lumerical FDTD.

My initial work involved reproducing and validating a published CBG design operating near 948.9 nm. Once the model had been validated, I translated the geometry to operate around 1300 nm, a wavelength of particular interest for optical-fibre-based quantum communication. I then investigated how the geometry influenced the optical performance, including the grating period, central radius, trench width, trench depth, number of rings, and membrane thickness. This provided a systematic understanding of the relationship between the physical structure and its electromagnetic behaviour.

Following this investigation, I developed two further designs, which we called Green Grass and Blue Sky. Green Grass represented a more fabrication-conscious approach, retaining a semi-etched CBG geometry while introducing a distributed Bragg reflector beneath the membrane. The purpose was to suppress downward photon emission and improve vertical extraction without completely departing from a conventional CBG structure. The optimised Green Grass design achieved approximately 94.3% upward emission, with downward emission reduced to approximately 0.15%.

Blue Sky was a more exploratory design that I independently proposed to investigate the theoretical performance of a less fabrication-constrained CBG architecture. Rather than retaining the partially etched membrane, I investigated a fully etched geometry with an air gap beneath the structure. This allowed me to explore whether removing some of the practical design constraints could significantly improve optical confinement and photon extraction. The final Blue Sky design achieved a simulated peak Purcell factor of approximately 30.17, a quality factor of 409.37, and an upward emission fraction of approximately 97.06%. These results demonstrated the potential benefits of more aggressive geometric optimisation, while also highlighting the distinction between idealised optical performance and practical fabrication feasibility.

Beyond electromagnetic optimisation, the project also investigated how cavity performance translates into the properties of a practical single-photon source. This involved considering the influence of radiative enhancement, phonon sidebands, photon purity, and indistinguishability, rather than treating extraction efficiency as the only measure of success. The resulting comparison demonstrated that maximising any individual figure of merit does not necessarily produce the most useful single-photon source.

The research was conducted primarily using Ansys Lumerical FDTD, supported by Python-based analysis scripts and high-performance computing resources. The project required extensive numerical simulations, automated parameter sweeps, spectral analysis, and comparisons between different mesh resolutions to assess the reliability of the results. I authored Chapter 6 of the final report and contributed to Chapters 1, 2, 9, and 11, alongside the wider cross-architecture analysis and discussion.

Looking back, this was one of the most substantial research projects I have undertaken. It gave me the opportunity to take responsibility for an entire photonic architecture, from reproducing published results to developing and evaluating my own design concepts. I particularly enjoyed the transition from validating established structures to proposing new geometries and investigating their physical limitations. The project strengthened my understanding of cavity quantum electrodynamics, nanophotonics, numerical electromagnetics, and the engineering challenges involved in developing quantum light sources.

Most importantly, it gave me valuable experience in independent scientific research, collaborative technical writing, and the process of turning theoretical ideas into quantitative simulation results.
